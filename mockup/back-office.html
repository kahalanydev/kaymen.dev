Admin · direction 1 of 3

Continuity the same building, one door further in

The premise

A client goes kaymen.dev → invite email → /portal, and Ohav goes kaymen.dev → /admin, several times a day. Three visual identities read as three vendors. This direction spends nothing on new ideas: the rail, the section rhythm, the hairline rules and the running-board row come over unchanged, and the project list literally is the marketing site's running board pointed at the projects table.

What changes

Only the palette and the components. Every screen, route and query stays where it is — this is admin/styles.css replaced and the class names in admin/app.js renamed. The six dashboard cards, Needs Attention, the activity feed and the leads list survive intact.

What it costs

The site's air is expensive in a tool. 74px section padding and a 760px prose measure are right for a page read once and wrong for a screen opened twenty times a day — below, the same information needs about 1.6 screens where today it needs one. It is also the least decisive of the three: it re-skins the dashboard without asking whether the dashboard was right.

Admin · direction 2 of 3

Triage-first the dashboard is a queue, not a report

The premise

On a normal morning the true answer is between zero and eight things. Today's dashboard makes you assemble that list yourself by scanning six cards — the metrics row, Needs Attention, Active Projects, Activity, Visitors and Contacts — and the list you assemble is the same one the server already computed. So compute it once, sort it, and put the action on the row: resolve, approve and reply without opening the object.

What changes

The six metrics compress into one sentence. Queue rows expand in place rather than navigating. Everything else — projects, clients, analytics, security — stays exactly as it is, one rail click away; this changes the landing screen only. Server-side it is one new aggregate endpoint over queries /api/admin/dashboard already runs.

What it costs

You lose the ambient numbers — the ones you notice without looking for them, like visitors doubling. And a queue is only as honest as the rules that fill it: a missing rule means a real problem never appears, and an empty queue is indistinguishable from a broken one. That needs a visible "last computed" and a rules list you can read.

Admin · direction 3 of 3

Dense console one operator, who knows it cold

The premise

This panel has exactly one daily user and he wrote it. Every navigation round-trip is a tax on someone who already knows where he is going. So: two panes — projects left, the selected project entire on the right — milestone and ticket rows at roughly a third the height of the site's board rows, tabular numerals so columns of figures align, sparklines instead of Chart.js, and ⌘K to jump to any project, ticket or org by name.

What changes

The most of the three. Project detail stops being a page you navigate to and becomes the right pane. The three stacked grids in admin/app.js — Milestones + Claude Code, Plan + Tickets, Members + Activity — collapse into one screen. The inline ticket status dropdown, which already exists, becomes the main way tickets move.

What it costs

Two real ones. It stops looking like kaymen.dev — a client who lands on /admin by accident sees a different product. And admin is an installable PWA with a bottom tabbar and card-tables; two panes have no phone layout, so mobile becomes a separately-designed surface rather than the same one narrowed. Also: the 224px rail is locked, and at this density it is the widest thing on screen doing the least — worth raising, not worth quietly shrinking.

Portal · direction 1 of 2

Reassurance answer the question before they scroll

The premise

A client logs in a couple of times a month and almost always for one reason: is my thing on track, and does anyone still care about it. Today they have to read a progress ring, count milestone dots and infer. So say it — in Sora, at the top, in a sentence — then show the evidence underneath. This is also the direct payoff of the No hostages section: the site promises transparency and this is where it either happens or does not.

What changes

Least of any direction. The ring, the phase bar and the milestone timeline all exist in portal/app.js — they get re-tinted, not rebuilt. The new part is a status sentence derived from data already in the dashboard response: next_milestone, days_remaining, milestones_done. Activity becomes dated prose instead of icon rows.

What it costs

A portal that only reassures is a portal nobody opens twice. Nothing here creates a reason to return, and nothing invites the client to tell you something — which is a real loss, because client tickets are the cheapest requirements you will ever get. It is also the direction that ages worst: the sentence has to stay true, so any project that quietly stalls now says so in 38px Sora.

Portal · direction 2 of 2

Workspace what is yours to do, beside where it stands

The premise

People return to places where they have an open loop, not to status pages. So the landing screen leads with the client's own to-do list — a plan awaiting their approval, a question we are blocked on, their open tickets — and puts progress in the side column. The compose box sits on the landing screen: reporting something should cost one action, because a client who does not report is a client who tells you at handover instead.

What changes

Tickets get promoted from a tab to the primary object, and plan approval moves out of its own page onto the front. Server-side this is close to free — /api/portal/dashboard already returns recentTickets, ticketStats, activeMilestones and the pending plan. Mostly it is a re-ordering of what the API already sends.

What it costs

It puts work on the client, and some clients read that as being managed by their vendor. And the empty state is load-bearing: a client with nothing to do lands on a blank left column, which reads as neglect exactly when things are going well. That state has to be designed properly, not left as a dash.

Email · server/utils/email.js

The re-skin one direction — email is not where you offer three

The premise

The wrapper is still #09090b with a #3b82f6 accent and JetBrains Mono — the theme the site dropped on 15 August. The wordmark already says kaymen, which makes it worse, not better: every invite, reset and ticket notification a client receives looks like a different company than the site they just came from, and the invite is usually the very first thing a new client ever gets from us.

What changes

One emailWrapper(), four bodies — invite, reset, new ticket, ticket resolved — and every one of them already routes through that single function. A --deep masthead carries the one dark band; the card is white; the accent appears exactly once per email, on the button. Tables and inline styles throughout.

What it costs

Almost nothing, and it is the smallest of the four jobs on this page. Two constraints are real though — see the notes under the previews. It should probably ship before either panel, because it is the surface a client sees first and it is a two-hour change.

Two constraints these previews are honest about
  • Sora will not render in most inboxes. Outlook, the Gmail app and Yahoo all ignore webfonts, so every heading above falls back to the system sans in the stack — which is why the design leans on size, weight and the dark masthead rather than on the face. What you see here in Chrome is the best case, not the common one.
  • Dark-mode clients will invert this. Apple Mail and Outlook auto-darken a white card and can force-invert text colours; the #16303d masthead survives that better than a white one, but the body still needs testing in a real dark client before it ships. The current dark wrapper is accidentally immune to this — it is the one thing being given up.
  • Everything is tables and inline styles, as the current wrapper already is. No <style> block, no flexbox, no CSS variables — Gmail strips the first and ignores the rest.
