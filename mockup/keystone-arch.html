The reference wins on shoulders, not on proportion

The Pennsylvania keystone is taller than it is wide and has a flat bottom. Those are the two properties I said were causing the cup reading, and it has both, and it still reads instantly as a stone. So the diagnosis needed correcting before drawing anything else.

Correcting my earlier read

What the reference actually has is shoulders. The sides flare outward below the top edge and only then taper in. No vessel can do that. A cup cannot bulge wider below its rim or you could not stack it, pour from it or hold it, so the shape stops being readable as one the moment the flare appears.

That is a better fix than either of mine, because it costs nothing: the proportion and the flat base both survive, and the letter can stay inside if you want it. One profile change, permanently.

First, the silhouette

Same three shapes at the same size. The only difference in the third is where the sides go between the top edge and the waist.

Your two asks, drawn

Dropping the K, and flanking the stone with its neighbours so it is visibly one stone in a course rather than a shape on its own.

The arch lockup

Wordmark in the span beneath the stone, three ways of reading "techy". Each is shown on light and on deep, and each is shown reduced, because the reduction is the part that has to survive: an arch with type inside it can never be a favicon.

What I would do

Take B as the mark and lockup C as the signature. They are the same object at two sizes, and that is not a figure of speech: B is literally the three centre segments of the ring that C draws, out of the same function at the same radii. The favicon and the letterhead are one drawing, not two that resemble each other. Flanking the stone, your second idea, is what makes the small size legible, so it is doing real work and not decoration.

A is the strongest shape on this page and it does not belong to that system. The shouldered silhouette is a stylisation, the way the Pennsylvania keystone is, rather than a segment of an actual ring, so it cannot be lifted out of the arch: the centre stone of a real arch has no shoulders. Shoulders and neighbours are two different answers to the same problem. Either is fine on its own. Using both is what I would avoid, because then the stone has two shapes and nothing is the mark.

One thing to decide, because it is a real question and not a nitpick. Putting kaymen.dev under the arch reads as us being the thing sheltered by it. The metaphor you liked runs the other way: we are the stone, and the client's business is what stays standing underneath. It is a stronger idea and it is also harder to use, because the wordmark has to go somewhere. C keeps the name under the span and puts the accent on the stone, which is the closest compromise. Worth knowing you are making that trade.

And the caveat that outlives all of this: an arch lockup is a signature, not a system. You still need the small mark, the deep-background variant and a one-colour version for anywhere that will not take a gradient. Those are drawn here rather than promised, which is why the reduction strip sits under every lockup.
